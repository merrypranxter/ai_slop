# Weird Sciences — Scientific Persona Palette for Visual Operations

Source: `originals/copilot-intake-2026-09-13/weird-sciences.pdf`

The most fertile scientific voices for this project are not merely famous scientists. They are
people attached to a destabilizing concept: cognition without brains, life without ordinary
DNA, organisms as worlds, bodies as electrical patterns, matter as computation, or evolution as
symbiotic takeover. These concepts translate directly into visual operations: networks,
membranes, recursive forms, alien semiotics, mineralized tissue, morphogenetic fields, and
organisms that are also machines.
The scientifically grounded material below should be treated as a palette, not as a claim that
every hypothesis is equally established. In particular, quantum consciousness and plant
magnetoreception remain contested, while slime-mold navigation, endosymbiosis, bioelectric
signaling, extremophile biology, and alternative biochemistry have much stronger empirical or
theoretical foundations.[ 2][ 4][^5]

Biological weirdos
Dussutourʼs work is especially useful because Physarum polycephalum is a giant single-celled
amoebozoan rather than a fungus or animal, yet it can navigate, adapt, and exhibit habituation-
like behavior. Nakagakiʼs slime-mold research gives the group a hard visual engine: search
fronts explore multiple routes, then retract inefficient paths while reinforcing useful transport
networks.[ 7][ 9][^10]
Levin adds a more radical body-horror vocabulary. His basal-cognition program investigates
how non-neural tissues use bioelectric signaling, feedback, and collective organization to
pursue anatomical goals during development and regeneration. The resulting image logic is not
“a brain inside a body,” but “the body is a distributed mind trying to become a body.”[ 12]
Margulis and Lovelock create an excellent argument pair. Margulis supplies the idea that
complex cells emerged through symbiotic mergers, while Lovelock supplies a planetary-scale
cybernetic view of life and environment. Put them together and ask for a scene in which every
organ is a treaty, every species is an organelle, and the planet is the disputed host.[ 5][^14]

Xenobiology is not simply “aliens.” It studies biological systems built with alternative
chemistry, including XNAs, noncanonical amino acids, and genetic firewalls that reduce
compatibility with ordinary life. This gives an art prompt a precise way to make alien biology
genuinely alien: change the information polymer, the molecular alphabet, the handedness, the
solvent, the energy source, or the rules of heredity.[ 4][^16]

Xenobiology and alien life
15][
NASAʼs astrobiology framework treats extremophiles as important analogues for possible life
elsewhere, including organisms adapted to high salt, extreme temperature, acidity or
alkalinity, heavy metals, high pressure, low water activity, and radiation. The most visually
interesting result is not a generic extraterrestrial animal; it is a biosphere whose architecture is
forced by a hostile chemical environment.[^3]

Research on fungal computing proposes that electrical activity in fungal networks may encode
signals, while later work explores how mycelial structures can function as adaptive
computational media. That does not mean mushrooms possess human-like minds; the stronger
and stranger prompt is to represent computation without a central processor: pulsing hyphae,
chemical thresholds, branching decisions, and fruit bodies as outputs.[^17]

Quantum biology is most useful when grounded in specific mechanisms rather than vague
“quantum magic.” Photosynthetic energy transfer and radical-pair magnetoreception are major
research candidates, while proposed quantum effects in smell, protein tunneling, and
membrane ion flow remain more speculative. Cryptochrome research explores how light-
driven radical pairs may respond to geomagnetic fields, but the exact biological role remains
an active scientific question.[ 18][ 20][^21]
Penrose and Hameroff can be included precisely because they are controversial. Their Orch OR
proposal links consciousness to quantum processes in neuronal microtubules and objective
reduction, but critical reviews emphasize the lack of convincing experimental evidence
connecting those processes to consciousness. Put a skeptical scientist in the room with them so
the generated prompts oscillate between sublime metaphysics and hard objections.[ 23]

Maturana and Varelaʼs theory of autopoiesis treats living systems as self-producing
organizations, while Varelaʼs enactive approach treats cognition as arising through an
organismʼs embodied coupling with its world. Uexküllʼs Umwelt is a particularly potent prompt
device: the same environment becomes radically different worlds when rendered from the
standpoint of a tick, moth, root, slime mold, or machine.[ 28][^29]
These are not historical people; they are deliberately fabricated round-table agents designed to
inject a specific scientific pressure into the conversation.
Dr. Morrow Coil, xenobiological taxonomist: Refuses to classify anything by Earth
categories; describes organisms by solvent, boundary behavior, energy gradient, and
method of self-copying.
The Mycelial Auditor: A fungal accountant that sees ecosystems as debts, exchanges,
abandoned pathways, and unpaid metabolic obligations.

Merlin Sheldrake, Andrew Adamatzky, Anna Tsing, Suzanne Simard, the Mycelial Auditor, and

humans merely project mysticism onto difficult chemistry. Force the final prompt to contain
both an exact mechanism, such as radical-pair spin dynamics, and one impossible
metaphysical consequence.[ 31][ 22]
DʼArcy Thompson, Alan Turing, René Thom, Ilya Prigogine, Benoît Mandelbrot, and Professor
Vanta Bloom should generate creatures whose bodies are equations made visible. Require
reaction-diffusion skins, catastrophe folds, fractal organs, logarithmic shells, and anatomy that
changes when the boundary conditions change.[ 25][ 33]
Use these as rules for the AI round table rather than as finished image prompts:
Every participant must contribute one scientifically grounded mechanism and one
impossible poetic mutation.
No generic “alien” forms; specify the organismʼs solvent, energy source, information
polymer, reproductive logic, sensory world, and environmental pressure.
Make anatomy emerge from process: growth, diffusion, folding, branching, pressure,
electrochemical gradients, symbiosis, or phase transition.
Require one scale collision per prompt: molecular machinery inside a landscape, a
planetary weather system inside a cell, or a city behaving like a slime mold.
Let the skeptic identify one scientifically weak assumption, then turn that weakness into a
deliberate surreal feature.
Ban ordinary eyes and mouths unless the organism has a compelling reason to evolve
them.
Ask each persona to describe what the scene feels like to the organism, not what it looks
like to a human observer.
Preserve disagreement. The best prompts should feel like an argument between
incompatible ontologies, not a single clean aesthetic.

Prompt directives for the room
1. “The body is a parliament”: A regenerative animal assembled from symbiotic bacterial
lineages, its organs debating through bioelectric voltage fields while a slime mold maps the
corridors of its mind.
2. “The forest has no center”: A fungal-electrical network computes weather, animal
migration, and human memory through pulsing hyphae; fruiting bodies appear as
temporary thoughts.
3. “The alien is a chemistry”: A methane-solvent organism stores heredity in an XNA-like
polymer and communicates by changing the mineral phase of its planetʼs surface.
4. “The bird sees the field”: A migratory birdʼs cryptochrome becomes a cathedral of radical
pairs, with blue photons, spin states, and geomagnetic lines forming a navigable interior
landscape.
5. “Turingʼs demon garden”: Reaction-diffusion waves generate animal markings, but every
stripe is also a doorway into a higher-dimensional morphogenetic space.
6. “The planet is an organelle”: A Gaia-like planetary metabolism is revealed as one cell in a
much larger symbiotic organism, with oceans functioning as cytoplasm and storms as
membrane potentials.
7. “Umwelt collision”: One meadow is shown simultaneously as a tickʼs three-signal universe,
a beeʼs ultraviolet cathedral, a fungusʼs chemical topology, and a humanʼs nostalgic
landscape.
8. “The protocell remembers”: Thousands of translucent vesicles assemble and dissolve in a
volcanic tidal pool, gradually forming a body plan that none of them individually contains.
9. “Fractal infection”: A parasite has no stable body; its anatomy repeats at every scale, from
molecular hijack to planetary ecology, with the host becoming a larger copy of the invader.
10. “The quantum séance”: A skeptical laboratory studies radical-pair magnetoreception while
an impossible organism appears only in the interval between measurement, decoherence,
and biological interpretation.
